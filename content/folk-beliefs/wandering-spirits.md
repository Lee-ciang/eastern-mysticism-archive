# Overview

Wandering spirits are a major idea in Chinese folk belief and ritual culture. They are often described as spirits without stable ancestral placement, descendants, offerings, burial, or ritual recognition. Some may be associated with accidental death, neglect, exile, poverty, violence, or social marginality. Interpretations vary widely by region and community.

The archive treats wandering spirits as cultural and religious figures, not as empirically verified beings. Their importance lies in how they structure rituals of compassion, protection, seasonal offering, and social memory.

# Difference from Ancestor Spirits

Wandering spirits are often contrasted with ancestor spirits. Ancestors are usually ritually incorporated into family life through tablets, household altars, tomb rites, offerings, and memorial days. Wandering spirits may lack this stable place. They may be imagined as hungry, unsettled, anonymous, or dangerous because they do not belong securely within a family or temple order.

This contrast is not simply supernatural. It reflects social concerns about family continuity, proper burial, poverty, violence, and the fate of those without descendants. Ritual attention to wandering spirits can express community responsibility toward the forgotten dead.

# Ghost Month

Ghost Month is one of the most important contexts for wandering-spirit beliefs. In many traditions, ghost gates are said to open, allowing spirits to move through the human world. Families and communities may make offerings of food, incense, paper money, or ritual performances to appease, feed, or acknowledge these spirits.

Public offerings during Ghost Month often include spirits who are not part of a specific lineage. This distinguishes them from ancestor rites, which focus on named family dead. The festival therefore extends ritual care beyond the household.

# Protection and Boundary Customs

Wandering spirits are also linked to protective practices. Some households avoid certain activities during Ghost Month, use threshold customs, or make offerings outside the home rather than inviting spirits inside. These practices should be understood as cultural boundary management rather than guaranteed protection.

Protective rituals, door customs, and talismans often respond to anxiety about unsettled spirits, impurity, or misfortune. They help communities mark the difference between welcome ancestors and potentially disruptive forces.
