# Overview

A household altar is a domestic ritual space used in Chinese folk religion, [ancestor veneration](/folk-beliefs/ancestor-veneration), and family religious practice. It may hold ancestor tablets, deity images, incense holders, lamps, flowers, fruit, tea, or other offerings.

The household altar shows how religious life can be centered in the home rather than only in temples. It turns ordinary domestic space into a place of remembrance, respect, protection, and ritual continuity.

# Historical Context

Household altars developed through the overlap of ancestor veneration, folk religion, Taoist influence, Buddhist influence, Confucian family ethics, and local custom. Their form can vary widely across regions and families.

Some altars are simple shelves with incense and ancestor tablets. Others are elaborate structures with multiple deities and ritual objects. Modern families may adapt altar practice to apartments, migration, safety rules, or changing religious commitments. There is no universal domestic layout.

# Ancestor Veneration

One common function of a household altar is honoring ancestors. Family members may offer incense, food, tea, fruit, or flowers before [ancestor tablets](/folk-beliefs/ancestor-tablets), photographs, or memorial images.

These practices express memory, gratitude, filial piety, and a sense of continuity between living and deceased family members.

The altar and tablet are not the same object. The altar is a setting for ritual attention; a tablet is one possible named memorial focus within it. Some families use photographs, plaques, or no permanent memorial object.

# Deities and Household Protection

Household altars may also include deities or protective figures. The [Kitchen God](/folk-beliefs/kitchen-god), for example, is associated with domestic morality and family life. [Door Gods](/folk-beliefs/door-gods) may be placed at entrances rather than on the altar, but they belong to the same broader world of household religious space.

This creates a network of domestic religious spaces: altar, kitchen, doorway, threshold, and ancestral memorial. Sharing a home does not make ancestors, deities, and protective figures the same category.

# Ritual Actions

Common ritual actions at a household altar may include lighting incense, bowing, placing offerings, cleaning the altar, replacing flowers, or marking festival days. These actions are usually guided by family custom rather than one universal rule. [Ancestor Ritual](/rituals/ancestor-ritual) describes possible sequences without prescribing a standard.

The archive presents these practices as cultural and religious traditions, not as instructions or guaranteed spiritual outcomes.

# Relationship to Festivals

Household altars can be important during festivals such as [Qingming](/folk-beliefs/qingming-festival), Ghost Month, Lunar New Year, death anniversaries, and memorial days. During these times, offerings may become more formal or abundant.

The altar helps connect seasonal time with family memory.

# Household Altar and Ancestral Hall

A household altar belongs to domestic space and usually serves one household or closely related family group. An [ancestral hall](/folk-beliefs/ancestral-hall) is a communal lineage institution that can preserve many tablets, genealogies, and collective rites. Neither should be treated as a universal requirement for ancestor remembrance.

# Cultural Significance

The household altar is a key bridge in the archive. It connects ancestor veneration, ancestor tablets, incense offering, spirit offerings, Kitchen God customs, door protection, and family ritual.
