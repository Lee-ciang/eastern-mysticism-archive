# Overview

Ancestor spirits are deceased family members understood within Chinese religious and ritual culture as continuing to matter to the living household, lineage, or community. They are central to ancestor veneration, household altars, ancestral halls, tablets, tomb rites, memorial offerings, and seasonal observances.

The archive treats ancestor spirits as cultural and religious entities. It does not claim that ancestors act in the world as a factual matter. Instead, it studies how ancestor-spirit beliefs organize family memory, ritual duty, lineage identity, mourning, and moral continuity.

# Ancestors and Family Continuity

Ancestor spirits are usually connected to named family lines. Through tablets, genealogies, altar practice, tomb care, and offerings, the dead can remain part of family life. This continuity reflects Confucian values of filial piety, but it also belongs to folk religion, Taoist ritual, and local custom.

Ancestor beliefs often support a layered view of time. The living inherit obligations from the past and transmit memory to future generations. Rituals make this continuity visible through incense, food, paper offerings, bowing, and commemorative dates.

# Difference from Wandering Spirits

Ancestor spirits are commonly distinguished from wandering spirits. Ancestors have a recognized place in family ritual. Wandering spirits may lack descendants, proper rites, or social recognition. This contrast reflects both religious imagination and social concern for the forgotten dead.

Ghost Month highlights the difference. Family ancestor rites may continue, but public offerings often address wandering spirits more broadly. Ghost Month and Ancestors is therefore an important bridge between lineage practice and communal care.

# Ritual Forms

Ancestor spirits are honored through ancestor ritual, household altars, ancestor tablets, Qingming tomb sweeping, funeral offerings, spirit offerings, and memorial observances. These practices vary by region, family background, and religious affiliation.

The Kitchen God may also appear in household religious life, though he is not an ancestor. His presence shows how domestic ritual can include ancestors, gods, and protective figures within one home-based religious environment.
