# Overview

Ancestor spirits are deceased family members understood within some Chinese religious and ritual traditions as continuing to matter to a living household, lineage, or community. This page focuses on ideas of ancestral presence, memory, continuity, and after-death family relationship. The broader practices belong to the [Ancestor Veneration](/folk-beliefs/ancestor-veneration) authority page.

The archive treats ancestor spirits as cultural and religious entities. It does not claim that ancestors act in the world as a factual matter. Instead, it studies how ancestor-spirit beliefs organize family memory, ritual duty, lineage identity, mourning, and moral continuity.

# Ancestors and Family Continuity

Ancestor spirits are usually connected to named family lines. Through [ancestor tablets](/folk-beliefs/ancestor-tablets), genealogies, altar practice, tomb care, and offerings, the dead can remain part of family life. This continuity reflects [filial piety](/folk-beliefs/filial-piety-and-ancestors), but it also belongs to folk religion, Taoist ritual, Buddhist memorial culture, and local custom.

Ancestor beliefs often support a layered view of time. The living inherit obligations from the past and transmit memory to future generations. Rituals make this continuity visible through incense, food, paper offerings, bowing, and commemorative dates.

# Difference from Wandering Spirits

Ancestor spirits are commonly distinguished from [wandering spirits](/folk-beliefs/wandering-spirits). Ancestors have a recognized place in family ritual through name, descent, memory, or a memorial site. Wandering spirits may be represented as lacking descendants, proper rites, or social recognition. This contrast reflects both religious imagination and social concern for forgotten or displaced dead.

[Ghost Month and Ancestors](/folk-beliefs/ghost-month-and-ancestors) highlights the difference. Family ancestor rites may continue, but public offerings often address wandering spirits more broadly. The categories can appear in the same festival without becoming identical.

# Difference from Deities

An ancestor belongs to a kinship relationship; a deity has a broader ritual identity that is not based on family descent. A [Kitchen God](/folk-beliefs/kitchen-god) may share a household ritual environment with ancestor tablets, yet the figure is not thereby transformed into a family ancestor.

Local traditions can create complex relationships among ancestors, culture heroes, lineage founders, and deified figures. The distinction should therefore be explained in context rather than reduced to the claim that every honored dead person is a god.

# Ritual Forms

Ancestor spirits may be addressed through [ancestor ritual](/rituals/ancestor-ritual), [household altars](/folk-beliefs/household-altar), tablets, Qingming tomb care, later memorial offerings, and lineage ceremonies. These practices vary by region, family background, migration history, historical period, and religious affiliation.

The Kitchen God may also appear in household religious life, though he is not an ancestor. His presence shows how domestic ritual can include ancestors, gods, and protective figures within one home-based religious environment.
