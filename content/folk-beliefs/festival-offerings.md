# Overview

Festival offerings are food, incense, paper goods, drink, fruit, candles, flowers, symbolic objects, or prepared dishes used during festival observances. They may be offered to ancestors, household gods, temple deities, wandering spirits, or communal ritual recipients depending on context.

In the Eastern Mysticism Archive, this page functions as a bridge between the Festival & Calendar Cluster and the existing ritual pages `spirit-offerings`, `temple-offerings`, `ancestor-ritual`, and `incense-offering`.

# Offerings in the Festival Cycle

Offerings appear across the `traditional-festival-cycle`. During `chinese-new-year-customs`, families may make offerings for renewal, ancestors, or household deities such as the Kitchen God. During `qingming-festival`, offerings are strongly connected with tomb sweeping and family remembrance. During `ghost-festival` and `ghost-month`, offerings may be made for ancestors, wandering spirits, or the hungry dead.

At `mid-autumn-festival`, offerings may involve moon symbolism, fruit, tea, incense, or mooncakes. During `winter-solstice-customs`, offerings may connect family reunion with seasonal return. `dragon-boat-festival` may include food customs and commemorative meanings.

# Ancestors and Spirit Recipients

Festival offerings are closely tied to `ancestor-veneration` and `ancestor-spirits`. Offerings can express filial piety, remembrance, gratitude, or the maintenance of relationship between living families and the dead.

The page `spirit-offerings` expands the topic beyond ancestors to spirits more generally. This is especially important for Ghost Festival customs, where offerings may be made for wandering or neglected spirits as well as family dead.

# Temple Offerings

`temple-offerings` explains how offerings may be placed in temple settings. Festival days can bring increased temple activity, deity observances, incense, public rites, and communal participation. A temple offering may be devotional, petitionary, thankful, protective, or commemorative.

Festival offerings therefore connect domestic practice with public religious space.

# Household Altars and Incense

Many offerings occur at a `household-altar`. A family altar can become especially active during festivals, anniversaries, lunar dates, and seasonal rites. `incense-offering` is often central because incense marks respect, attention, communication, and ritual presence.

Food and incense together create a visible language of offering. One nourishes symbolically; the other marks ritual address.

# Spirit Money and Paper Goods

`spirit-money` is another important related concept. Paper goods may be burned in some traditions so that symbolic value is transferred to the spirit world. This practice is especially associated with ancestor rites, funerary customs, Ghost Month, and some festival observances.

The archive treats these acts as cultural and religious practices rather than literal claims.

# Cultural Context

Festival offerings vary greatly by region, temple, family, economic circumstance, and local custom. Some communities emphasize elaborate ritual tables. Others use simple fruit, tea, incense, or remembered family foods.

Modern families may understand offerings religiously, symbolically, emotionally, or culturally. The practice can preserve memory even when belief is interpreted in different ways.

Offerings also help connect different festivals into one recognizable cycle. A family may use different foods and ritual objects for New Year, Qingming, Ghost Festival, Mid-Autumn, or Winter Solstice, but the underlying pattern of respectful preparation remains familiar. This repetition makes festival offerings one of the clearest bridges between calendar time, ancestor memory, household ritual, and temple culture.
