# Overview

Door gods are protective figures placed at entrances in Chinese folk religion. They are commonly represented as paired guardians on doors, gates, prints, or temple entrances.

Their role is to symbolically guard thresholds and mark the entrance as a protected boundary.

# Protective Meaning

Doors are important in many religious and cultural systems because they separate inside from outside, family from public space, and order from uncertainty. Door gods express this boundary symbolically.

In folk belief, they are associated with warding off harmful influences, misfortune, or unwanted spirits. The archive presents these meanings as traditional beliefs rather than factual guarantees.

# Visual Forms

Door gods are often depicted as martial figures, officials, or guardian heroes. Their appearance may include armor, weapons, stern expressions, and symmetrical placement.

The paired arrangement reinforces the idea of balanced protection at the threshold.

# Seasonal Customs

Door god images are often renewed during Lunar New Year preparations. Replacing or posting images can be part of a broader cycle of cleaning, renewal, and protection for the household.

These customs vary widely by region and family practice.

# Relationship to Household Religion

Door gods belong to a larger world of household protective practices that may include talismans, red paper, incense, altar offerings, and Kitchen God customs.

They show how folk religion treats the home as a spiritually meaningful space.

# Cultural Significance

Door gods remain recognizable in Chinese visual culture, temple art, New Year prints, and protective symbolism. They are foundational for understanding threshold protection and household religious practice.
