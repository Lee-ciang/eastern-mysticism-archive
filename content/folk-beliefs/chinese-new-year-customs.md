# Overview

Chinese New Year customs mark the opening of the lunar year through family gathering, renewal, food, offerings, protective symbols, greetings, and household preparation. The festival is widely known for celebration, but it also belongs to a deeper ritual cycle involving ancestors, domestic gods, auspicious language, cleaning, protection, and the symbolic resetting of the year.

In this archive, Chinese New Year customs are treated as cultural and historical practices. Local observances vary across regions, dialect communities, religions, families, and diasporic settings.

# New Year in the Festival Cycle

Within the Festival & Calendar Cluster, `chinese-new-year-customs` links strongly to `traditional-festival-cycle` and `lunar-calendar`. It begins the yearly sequence that later includes `qingming-festival`, `dragon-boat-festival`, `ghost-festival`, `mid-autumn-festival`, and `winter-solstice-customs`.

The New Year is not only a beginning in numerical time. It is also a ritual beginning. Families may clean the home, settle obligations, prepare food, place decorations, visit relatives, honor ancestors, and mark the household as renewed.

# Ancestors and Household Continuity

Ancestor-related practice can be part of New Year observance. Families may make offerings at a household altar, remember deceased relatives, or invite ancestral blessing into the renewed year. This connects Chinese New Year with `ancestor-veneration`, `ancestor-spirits`, `household-altar`, and `spirit-offerings`.

The New Year can therefore express continuity as much as novelty. The family moves into a new cycle while remaining connected to those who came before.

# Kitchen God and Domestic Reporting

The `kitchen-god` is especially important in some New Year traditions. Popular customs may describe the Kitchen God reporting household conduct to Heaven near the end of the lunar year. Offerings may be made before his departure and return.

This links New Year practice to household morality, domestic ritual, and the wider idea of celestial order. It also connects to `temple-offerings`, because some families combine home observance with temple visits.

# Protection, Doors, and Auspicious Renewal

Chinese New Year customs often include protective and auspicious imagery. Door couplets, red paper, door gods, lanterns, and blessing characters may mark thresholds and invite good fortune. These customs connect to `door-gods`, `household-protection`, `protective-folk-customs`, and `incense-offering`.

The doorway becomes ritually important because it stands between inside and outside, old and new, danger and blessing.

# Offerings and Food

New Year food can carry symbolic meaning. Dishes may express abundance, reunion, longevity, sweetness, or prosperity through sound, shape, number, or family memory. `festival-offerings`, `spirit-offerings`, and `temple-offerings` help explain how food may operate both socially and ritually.

Not every New Year meal is a religious act, but many food customs preserve older symbolic layers.

# Relationship to Other Festivals

Chinese New Year sets the tone for the broader year. `qingming-festival` later turns attention toward tombs and ancestors in spring. `ghost-festival` and `ghost-month` address spirits and offerings in the seventh lunar month. `mid-autumn-festival` centers the moon and reunion. `winter-solstice-customs` closes the year with seasonal return and family gatherings.

Together these observances form a cultural calendar of renewal, remembrance, protection, and reunion.

# Cultural Context

Modern Chinese New Year may be celebrated as a family holiday, public festival, religious observance, cultural identity marker, or seasonal break. Its practices can include temple visits, fireworks, red envelopes, reunion dinners, ancestor offerings, greetings, cleaning, decorations, and visits.

The archive emphasizes the ritual and symbolic dimensions because they reveal how festival customs connect household life with the larger Festival & Calendar Cluster.
