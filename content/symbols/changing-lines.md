# Overview

Changing lines, also called moving lines, are lines in an I Ching reading that transform from Yin to Yang or from Yang to Yin. They are central to the divinatory logic of the Book of Changes because they turn a hexagram into a dynamic process rather than a fixed image.

In a reading, the first hexagram describes the present pattern or situation. Changing lines identify points of movement, tension, or transformation. When those lines change, they can produce a second hexagram that suggests development or direction.

# Line Change and Transformation

The I Ching is a text about change. `changing-lines` make that principle visible. A stable line may remain as it is, while a changing line indicates movement. This movement may be interpreted as warning, opportunity, transition, pressure, release, or a shift in the situation.

This directly connects the page to `hexagrams`, because changing lines alter the hexagram pattern. It also connects to `yin-yang`, because the line changes between Yin and Yang forms.

# Yin, Yang, and Balance

Changing lines are a practical example of Yin-Yang transformation. A broken line may become unbroken, and an unbroken line may become broken. This movement reflects the wider archive themes of `yin-and-yang-balance`, `dao`, and `cosmology-in-taoism`.

Balance in this context is not stillness. It is a responsive relationship between changing forces.

# Methods That Produce Changing Lines

Different `divination-methods` produce changing lines in different ways. The `yarrow-stalk-method` traditionally generates line values through a longer process. `casting-coins` creates line values through coin combinations. Both can produce stable and moving lines.

The method matters because it shapes how the hexagram is formed. It also creates a ritual frame around the question.

# Trigrams and Hexagram Movement

When changing lines alter a hexagram, they may also alter its trigrams. This affects the images and relationships within the reading. The pages `trigrams` and `eight-trigrams-divination` help explain why this matters.

For example, changing a line may shift the lower or upper trigram into a different image, creating a new symbolic relation.

# Sequence and Interpretation

The resulting hexagram can be read in relation to the `king-wen-sequence`. Sequence, paired hexagrams, reversal, and contrast can all contribute to interpretation. The I Ching reading becomes a movement between signs rather than one isolated answer.

This connects changing lines to `oracle-traditions`, where symbolic systems often depend on timing, transformation, and careful interpretation.
