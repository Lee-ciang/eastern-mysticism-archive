# Overview

Qi circulation refers to traditional ideas about the movement, gathering, and transformation of qi. In Taoist practice and Chinese cosmological language, qi is often described as something that can flow, settle, rise, descend, disperse, or become blocked.

This archive presents qi circulation as a cultural and historical concept. It does not provide medical, therapeutic, or instructional claims.

# Historical Context

Ideas of qi circulation appear in many Chinese traditions, including Taoist cultivation, ritual practice, martial arts culture, medicine, meditation, and Feng Shui. These fields use different vocabularies and methods, but they often share the assumption that movement and pattern matter.

In Taoist contexts, circulation may be linked to breath, bodily awareness, inner refinement, ritual preparation, and symbolic alignment with the Dao.

# Circulation as Pattern

The term circulation suggests movement through a system. Traditional descriptions may speak of qi moving through the body, through a landscape, through a room, or through the seasons. These descriptions are interpretive models for understanding relationship and flow.

In the body, qi circulation is often discussed in relation to cultivation traditions. In Feng Shui, qi flow refers to how space is interpreted. In cosmology, qi circulation can describe broader patterns of transformation.

# Relationship to Yin Yang

Qi circulation is often described through Yin Yang qualities. Movement and stillness, expansion and contraction, warmth and coolness, ascent and descent can all be used to interpret qi.

Balanced circulation is not simply constant movement. Too much motion may be interpreted as scattered, while too much stillness may be interpreted as stagnant.

# Relationship to Five Elements

The Five Elements provide another way to understand circulation. Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water describe phases of movement and change. Qi circulation may be interpreted as passing through these qualities in seasonal, bodily, spatial, or symbolic contexts.

This creates a bridge between qi and broader Chinese correlative systems.

# Taoist Practice

In Taoist cultivation traditions, qi circulation may be described in relation to breath, posture, attention, inner alchemy, or meditative discipline. Specific methods vary widely by lineage and historical context.

The archive does not teach techniques. It explains the concept as part of Taoist cultural and religious history.

# Feng Shui Parallel

Qi circulation has a spatial counterpart in Feng Shui. A house, room, road, river, or mountain formation may be interpreted through patterns of flow. This is why `qi-circulation` and `qi-flow` should be linked but not collapsed into the same entry.

# Cultural Significance

Qi circulation is an important cluster topic because it connects Taoist practice, Feng Shui, Yin Yang, Five Elements, and ideas of balanced qi. It helps explain how qi became a practical interpretive concept rather than only an abstract term.
