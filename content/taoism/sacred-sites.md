# Overview

Sacred sites are places treated as religiously, ritually, historically, or symbolically significant. In Chinese and Taoist contexts, sacred sites may include temples, mountains, ancestral halls, graves, springs, caves, shrines, processional routes, old trees, bridges, gates, or landscapes associated with deities and immortals.

The archive uses Sacred Sites as a broad category of place-based entities. It does not claim that a site has supernatural force as a factual matter. Instead, it studies how communities assign meaning to places through ritual, memory, story, architecture, and repeated practice.

# Types of Sacred Sites

Taoist temples are among the most visible sacred sites. They contain halls, altars, courtyards, incense spaces, and deity images. Sacred mountains are another major type, especially where pilgrimage routes and temple complexes developed over time.

Ancestral halls and tombs are sacred in a different sense. They connect place with lineage, memory, filial piety, and ancestor veneration. Some folk religious sites may focus on local gods, protective customs, or seasonal festivals.

# Ritual Landscape

Sacred sites are not isolated from their surroundings. Paths, gates, thresholds, water, elevation, orientation, and neighboring buildings can all shape the ritual experience. Feng Shui ideas may influence how people interpret landforms, Qi flow, direction, and placement.

In this sense, a sacred site is both a location and a relationship. It connects people, land, memory, deities, ancestors, and ritual time.

# Community Function

Sacred sites often serve as community centers. They host festivals, offerings, processions, purification rites, protection rituals, ancestor observances, and public gatherings. Donations, inscriptions, repairs, and local stories help maintain continuity across generations.

Because sacred sites accumulate memory, they are important for the archive's knowledge graph. They can link concepts, deities, rituals, landscapes, and social groups.
