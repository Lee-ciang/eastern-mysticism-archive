# Overview

Afterlife beliefs in Chinese religious traditions include ideas about ancestors, souls, underworld courts, rebirth, ghostly wandering, ritual care, and moral consequence. These beliefs vary widely across region, period, class, temple tradition, and family practice. They are best understood as a broad cultural field rather than a single doctrine.

Taoist traditions contributed ritual systems, cosmological language, spirit registers, and liturgies for the dead. Buddhist influence contributed concepts of karmic judgment, hells, rebirth, and merit transfer. Confucian family ethics emphasized filial duty, mourning, memorial rites, and the continuing importance of ancestors. Folk religion joined these ideas to household altars, festivals, local gods, and protective customs.

# Souls and Continuity

Some Chinese traditions speak of multiple aspects of the person after death. The paired concepts of Hun and Po are especially important. They are often interpreted as different soul aspects, with the Hun associated with more subtle or ascending qualities and the Po with bodily or earthly aspects. Interpretations vary, and the terms should not be reduced to a single fixed definition.

Ancestor beliefs emphasize continuity between generations. The dead may become honored ancestors when properly remembered and ritually incorporated into family life. This differs from the idea of wandering spirits, who may be imagined as unsettled, neglected, or lacking descendants.

# Judgment and Transition

Afterlife imagery often includes judgment, registration, and transition. Underworld courts and the Ten Kings of Hell present the postmortem journey as a moral and bureaucratic process. These images are religious representations used in ritual, art, and teaching, not factual claims made by the archive.

The concept of judgment after death links conduct, ritual care, and cosmic order. It can support moral instruction while also giving families a framework for funeral rites, memorial observances, and offerings.

# Ritual Practice

Afterlife beliefs are closely tied to practice. Funeral offerings, incense, food, paper money, scriptures, chanting, ritual petitions, and ancestor tablets all create ways for living communities to care for the dead. These customs may express grief, filial responsibility, social continuity, and religious hope.

Ghost Month and Qingming are especially important seasonal contexts. Ghost Month may focus on wandering spirits and the opening of ghost gates, while Qingming emphasizes tomb sweeping and ancestor remembrance.
