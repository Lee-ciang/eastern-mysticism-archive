# Overview

Cosmology in Taoism refers to the way Taoist traditions describe the structure, movement, and symbolic order of reality. It includes ideas about the Dao, qi, Yin and Yang, Heaven and Earth, the Three Realms, celestial beings, natural harmony, and the patterned relationship between human life and the wider universe.

In this archive, cosmology is treated as a cultural, religious, and philosophical subject. Taoist cosmology is not presented as a scientific model of the universe. It is a symbolic and interpretive system that shaped ritual, meditation, temple culture, diagrams, sacred geography, and moral imagination across Chinese traditions.

# The Dao as Cosmological Ground

The Dao is the central principle behind Taoist cosmology. It is not simply a deity, object, or place. It is the way, source, pattern, and process through which the world unfolds. Many cosmological pages in this archive connect back to `dao` because Taoist explanations of existence often begin with a reality deeper than named forms.

The Dao gives cosmology its philosophical depth. It explains why Taoist thought often values naturalness, non-forcing, transformation, and responsive balance. The universe is not imagined as a dead mechanism, but as a living pattern of change.

# Qi, Movement, and Transformation

Qi is one of the most important concepts in Taoist cosmology. It describes vital energy, breath, material force, and dynamic presence. Pages such as `qi`, `qi-energy`, `qi-circulation`, and `qi-flow` show how qi connects body, landscape, ritual space, and cosmic order.

In Taoist interpretation, qi is not isolated inside the human body. It circulates through Heaven and Earth, gathers in places, transforms through seasons, and appears in ritual, meditation, medicine, Feng Shui, and symbolic diagrams. This makes qi one of the strongest bridges between Taoist cosmology and everyday cultural practice.

# Yin, Yang, and Balance

Yin and Yang explain polarity, alternation, and mutual dependence. They appear in the `yin-yang` symbol, in `yin-yang-cosmology`, and in the broader idea of `yin-and-yang-balance`. Taoist cosmology uses Yin and Yang to describe how apparent opposites generate movement and relation.

Dark and bright, still and active, receptive and expressive, hidden and manifest are not fixed enemies. They are paired tendencies within a larger flow. This way of thinking is one reason Taoist cosmology often emphasizes balance rather than conquest.

# Five Elements and Symbolic Order

The Five Elements, or Five Phases, add another layer of cosmological explanation. The page `five-elements` introduces the symbolic system, while `five-elements-theory` focuses on its broader interpretive role. Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water are not merely physical substances. They are patterns of growth, heat, stability, contraction, and fluidity.

These phases connect cosmology to ritual timing, medicine, music, direction, color, seasons, and Feng Shui. They also intersect with `bagua`, which organizes directions and symbolic qualities through trigrams.

# Heaven, Earth, and Humanity

Taoist cosmology often describes existence through the relationship between Heaven, Earth, and humanity. The pages `heaven-and-earth` and `heaven-earth-human` are core nodes for this theme. Heaven may refer to celestial order, Earth to grounded manifestation, and humanity to the responsive position between them.

This relationship is not only theoretical. It appears in ritual architecture, ancestor rites, temple offerings, cultivation practices, and moral language about living in alignment with the world.

# Realms, Heavens, and Sacred Administration

Religious Taoism also developed rich images of layered realms, divine offices, and celestial spaces. The pages `three-realms`, `taoist-heavens`, `jade-emperor`, and `cosmic-order` help explain this side of the cluster. Taoist cosmology can include abstract principles and populated sacred worlds at the same time.

This combination is important. Taoism is not only philosophy, and it is not only temple religion. Its cosmological imagination often moves between both registers.

# Cultural Context

Cosmology shaped how people interpreted mountains, bodies, homes, temples, calendars, offerings, symbols, and spiritual authority. It helped explain why ritual gestures mattered, why directions were meaningful, why harmony was valued, and why the human world was understood as connected to unseen patterns.

Cosmology in Taoism is therefore the central hub for the archive's cosmology cluster. It links philosophical concepts, symbolic systems, religious imagery, and cultural practices into one interpretive field.
