# Overview

Temple altars are ritual surfaces and focal areas used for offerings, incense, lamps, vessels, flowers, food, petitions, scriptures, images, and ceremonial tools. In Taoist and Chinese temple culture, altars help organize the relationship between worshippers, ritual specialists, deities, ancestors, and other spirit-world figures.

The archive treats temple altars as cultural and religious objects. It does not present altar practice as producing guaranteed supernatural outcomes. Their importance lies in how they structure ritual action, visual hierarchy, and devotional attention.

# Altar Function

An altar can be a place for presenting offerings, lighting incense, placing ritual documents, arranging symbolic objects, or marking the presence of a deity. In larger temples, different halls may contain different altars for principal deities, side deities, guardians, ancestors, or local gods.

Altar organization often reflects hierarchy. A main altar may face the entrance or stand before a principal image. Side altars may hold supporting figures. Ritual specialists may use temporary altars during festivals, funerary rites, purification ceremonies, or protection rituals.

# Objects on Temple Altars

Common altar objects include incense burners, candles or lamps, offering bowls, flowers, fruit, ritual vessels, bells, scriptures, tablets, and written petitions. The arrangement varies by temple, region, and tradition.

Incense is especially important because it marks ritual presence and respect. Offerings may express gratitude, petition, remembrance, hospitality, or fulfillment of a vow.

# Relation to Deities and Ancestors

Temple altars may serve deities such as the Jade Emperor or other figures in Taoist pantheons. They may also be used for ancestral rites or public rituals directed toward spirits. The specific recipient matters because offerings to deities, ancestors, and wandering spirits can carry different meanings.
