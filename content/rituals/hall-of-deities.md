# Overview

The hall of deities is a temple space where images, statues, tablets, paintings, or other representations of gods and sacred figures are displayed. In Taoist and Chinese temple culture, such halls organize devotion, ritual hierarchy, offerings, and the visual presence of the divine order.

The archive treats deity halls as cultural and religious spaces. It does not present temple images as factual embodiments of supernatural beings. Their importance lies in iconography, ritual focus, communal memory, and the way temples make theology visible.

# Deity Arrangement

A hall may contain a principal deity at the center, with attendant figures, guardians, or secondary deities placed nearby. The Jade Emperor, the Three Pure Ones, Lord Lao, local gods, thunder deities, immortals, or other figures may appear depending on the temple's dedication.

The arrangement often reflects hierarchy. Central placement, height, size, lighting, banners, and altar position can signal importance. Side halls may contain related gods, local protective figures, or ancestral elements.

# Ritual Focus

The hall of deities is where visitors may bow, offer incense, present petitions, give thanks, or participate in ceremonies. Temple altars and incense burners help mediate these actions.

Ritual specialists may conduct liturgies before deity images, especially during festivals, purification rites, protection rituals, and public ceremonies.

# Cosmological Meaning

Deity halls link temple space to Taoist heavens and celestial bureaucracy. The temple becomes a miniature sacred order, with divine offices arranged in visible form. This structure helps visitors understand relationships among deities, ancestors, spirits, and ritual authorities.

The hall also connects sacred architecture with narrative. Images and inscriptions may tell stories about gods, immortals, moral teachings, or local temple history.
