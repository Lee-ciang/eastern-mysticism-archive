# Overview

Temple courtyards are open spaces within or around Chinese temple complexes. They may serve as gathering areas, incense zones, processional routes, festival spaces, transitional thresholds, and practical buffers between gates and halls.

Although courtyards are architectural features, they are also ritual spaces. They shape how visitors move, where offerings are prepared, how crowds gather, and how the main hall is approached.

# Spatial Role

Courtyards often sit between an entrance gate and a hall of deities. This placement creates a gradual transition from the outside world into a more focused ritual setting. Visitors may pause, light incense, bow, consult temple staff, or prepare offerings before entering a hall.

In larger temples, multiple courtyards can create layers of access. Each layer may mark a shift in importance, quietness, or ritual intensity.

# Ritual Activities

Courtyards may contain incense burners, offering tables, festival equipment, drums, temporary altars, processional objects, or spaces for public performance. During major festivals, they can become crowded centers of activity.

Temple courtyards are also useful for purification and protection practices. Open space allows ritual specialists to manage fire, smoke, sound, movement, and ceremonial boundaries.

# Architecture and Feng Shui

Courtyards contribute to light, air, circulation, visibility, and orientation. In some interpretations, they may be discussed through Feng Shui concepts such as Qi flow, directional balance, and the relationship between building and landscape.

This archive treats such interpretations as cultural frameworks. It does not claim that a courtyard arrangement guarantees particular outcomes.
