# Overview

Temple incense refers to incense used in temple settings for worship, offerings, purification, petitions, remembrance, and public ritual. It is one of the most recognizable practices in Taoist temples, folk religious shrines, ancestral halls, and mixed Chinese religious spaces.

This archive treats incense as a ritual and symbolic medium. It does not present smoke as factual proof of communication with spirits or deities. Its importance comes from the meanings communities give to scent, smoke, gesture, offering, and ritual presence.

# Temple Practice

Visitors may light incense before entering a main hall, at courtyard burners, before deity images, or near side altars. Some temples guide worshippers through a sequence of incense stations. Others encourage simpler offerings depending on local custom, safety rules, or temple management.

Incense can mark respect for deities, ancestors, or other ritual recipients. It may accompany bowing, silent prayer, petitions, vows, thanks, or memorial acts.

# Courtyards and Burners

Temple courtyards often contain large incense burners. These spaces allow smoke, movement, and crowds to circulate. In some temples, incense is offered outside the hall before visitors proceed toward the hall of deities.

The placement of incense burners helps define the boundary between ordinary movement and ritual focus. It also links temple architecture to ritual action.

# Symbolic Meaning

Incense smoke is often interpreted as rising intention, purification, respect, or the movement of offerings toward invisible realms. Such interpretations vary by tradition and should be understood as religious symbolism rather than empirical fact.

Incense also creates sensory continuity. The smell, smoke, ash, and repeated gestures help temples feel like long-standing places of devotion.
