# Knowledge Asset Production Framework (KAPF) v1.0

**Status:** Official operating framework  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Scope:** All current and future knowledge projects  
**Audience:** Project owners, editors, strategists, developers, designers, analysts, and AI collaborators

## 1. Introduction

The Knowledge Asset Production Framework (KAPF) is a reusable operating system for building durable knowledge projects. It applies to educational archives, specialist publishing sites, reference libraries, product knowledge bases, and other projects whose value depends on trustworthy, interconnected information. KAPF is intentionally domain-independent. It can guide projects such as Eastern Mysticism Archive, Sleep Project, PilatesHaven, POE2, and future knowledge websites without imposing one editorial voice, technology stack, or commercial model. The framework treats every article, diagram, dataset, internal link, taxonomy rule, and operating document as a knowledge asset. A knowledge asset should have a defined purpose, an accountable source, a quality standard, and a place within a larger system. KAPF replaces ad hoc publishing with a staged production model. Each stage has entry criteria, exit criteria, expected deliverables, and validation requirements. Projects may move through the framework at different speeds, but they should not skip foundational controls merely to increase output. The framework is designed to support both human-led and AI-assisted production. Final responsibility remains with the project owner and designated human reviewers.

## 2. Philosophy

KAPF is built on a simple premise: useful knowledge compounds when it is structured, connected, maintained, and reusable. A page is not an isolated publication. It is a node in a knowledge graph, a source for future formats, and a long-term interface between a subject and its audience. The framework therefore optimizes for cumulative authority rather than short-lived output volume. It favors deliberate systems over campaigns, reusable assets over disposable production, and verified understanding over superficial coverage. The work begins with user needs and domain structure. Search demand, competitive opportunity, and distribution channels inform priorities, but they do not replace editorial judgment. Evergreen assets receive preference because their value can grow through revision, linking, visual reuse, and adaptation. Trending material may be included when it serves the project's mission and can be maintained responsibly. KAPF views documentation as operational infrastructure. Standards should be recorded before they must be enforced at scale. Decisions should be traceable, repeatable, and understandable by collaborators who were not present when those decisions were made. Quality is defined as fitness for purpose across accuracy, clarity, structure, accessibility, discoverability, and maintainability. No single metric is allowed to substitute for this full definition.

## 3. Core Principles

### 3.1 Documentation First

Document recurring decisions, formats, and review rules before expanding production. Documentation should be concise enough to use and specific enough to audit. Standards must evolve through explicit versioning rather than undocumented drift.

### 3.2 One Source, Multiple Outputs

One authoritative source should support multiple appropriate outputs. An article may inform a diagram, social asset, lesson, glossary entry, video script, newsletter, or structured dataset. Derived outputs must preserve the meaning and limitations of the source.

### 3.3 Data Before Expansion

Expansion should respond to evidence from users, search performance, graph analysis, editorial gaps, and strategic goals. Production volume alone is not evidence of progress.

### 3.4 Quality Before Quantity

Publish fewer assets when necessary to preserve accuracy, coherence, and maintenance capacity. Scale only after the workflow can reproduce the required standard.

### 3.5 Evergreen Before Trending

Build durable foundations before allocating substantial resources to volatile topics. Time-sensitive content must have an owner, review date, and retirement or update plan.

### 3.6 Reusable Assets First

Prefer assets that can support multiple pages, channels, or future products. Taxonomies, diagrams, timelines, maps, templates, and structured research notes often have greater compounding value than one-use media.

### 3.7 Graph Before Inventory

Content count is secondary to useful relationships among assets. Every new entity should have a defined role within a topic cluster and the wider knowledge graph.

### 3.8 Human Accountability

AI may accelerate research, drafting, analysis, validation, and transformation. Humans retain responsibility for strategy, factual judgment, sensitive interpretation, approval, and publication.

### 3.9 Validation by Default

Every production workflow must end with checks proportional to its risk. Validation should be automated where possible and reviewed by humans where judgment is required.

### 3.10 Maintainability as a Feature

An asset is incomplete if the team cannot update, verify, or retire it efficiently. Clear ownership, predictable structure, and recorded provenance are part of asset quality.

## 4. The Six KAPF Phases

### Phase 1: Foundation

Foundation establishes the project's purpose, audience, technical baseline, editorial rules, and measurement model. Typical outputs include a project charter, domain scope, taxonomy draft, content template, technical architecture, metadata standard, analytics setup, and governance documents. The objective is not to predict every future need. It is to establish enough shared structure that early work remains coherent and reversible.

### Phase 2: Knowledge Graph

Knowledge Graph organizes the subject into entities, relationships, clusters, hubs, bridges, and supporting pages. The phase converts a list of topics into an intentional information network. Typical outputs include an entity inventory, cluster map, hub definitions, linking rules, slug registry, coverage matrix, and graph-health baseline.

### Phase 3: Authority Layer

Authority Layer deepens the most important pages until they can serve as durable reference points. Authority pages explain foundational concepts, resolve common questions, establish terminology, and connect readers to narrower entities. Typical outputs include cornerstone articles, expert review notes, source records, FAQs, contextual internal links, and update schedules.

### Phase 4: Visual Knowledge System

Visual Knowledge System defines and produces images that improve understanding rather than merely decorate pages. Typical outputs include visual standards, reusable diagrams, relationship maps, timelines, comparisons, maps, illustrations, accessibility text, and asset metadata. The phase also establishes production, review, naming, storage, and version-control workflows.

### Phase 5: Data Driven Growth

Data Driven Growth uses measured behavior and graph evidence to prioritize improvements. Typical inputs include search performance, engagement, conversion, navigation paths, internal-link data, content freshness, accessibility results, and production costs. Typical outputs include prioritized refreshes, linking improvements, experiment briefs, opportunity scores, and retirement decisions.

### Phase 6: Knowledge Asset Expansion

Knowledge Asset Expansion adapts validated knowledge into additional formats, channels, audiences, languages, products, and services. Possible outputs include newsletters, courses, books, videos, downloadable references, tools, datasets, translations, APIs, and licensed assets. Expansion should preserve the authoritative source and maintain traceable relationships among derivatives.

## 5. Entry Criteria for Each Phase

### Phase 1 Entry Criteria

- A defined project sponsor or owner exists.
- The intended audience and problem are stated.
- The domain is sufficiently bounded for an initial release.
- Legal, ethical, and safety constraints have been identified.
- A repository or controlled workspace is available.
- Initial time, budget, and maintenance capacity are understood.

### Phase 2 Entry Criteria

- The project charter has been approved.
- Publishing and technical conventions are documented.
- A stable content format exists.
- Initial domain research is available.
- Core categories and terminology have provisional definitions.
- Duplicate and broken-link checks can be performed.

### Phase 3 Entry Criteria

- Core entities and clusters are mapped.
- Hub and supporting-page roles are assigned.
- High-value foundational topics are identified.
- Internal linking rules are documented.
- Baseline graph health is known.
- Research and review standards are operational.

### Phase 4 Entry Criteria

- Priority authority pages are stable enough to illustrate.
- Visual use cases have educational objectives.
- Brand, accessibility, and file standards are documented.
- Subject-matter review is available for diagrams and symbols.
- Storage, naming, and version-control conventions exist.
- Image integration will not undermine performance targets.

### Phase 5 Entry Criteria

- Reliable analytics and search data are being collected.
- The site has enough content or usage to reveal patterns.
- Baseline technical and graph metrics exist.
- Data quality limitations are understood.
- The team can distinguish observations from causal claims.
- There is capacity to act on findings.

### Phase 6 Entry Criteria

- The source knowledge base has demonstrated quality and demand.
- Authority assets have clear ownership and update processes.
- Target formats or channels have defined audiences.
- Rights, licensing, attribution, and privacy issues are resolved.
- Production costs and maintenance obligations are estimated.
- Expansion will not degrade the core knowledge product.

## 6. Exit Criteria for Each Phase

### Phase 1 Exit Criteria

- Project vision, audience, scope, and exclusions are documented.
- Content, metadata, accessibility, and technical standards are approved.
- Measurement and governance responsibilities are assigned.
- A repeatable publishing workflow has passed a pilot.
- The initial release can be built, validated, and maintained.
- Major unresolved risks are recorded with owners.

### Phase 2 Exit Criteria

- Core topic clusters have defined hubs and supporting entities.
- Slugs are unique and stable.
- Required links resolve correctly.
- No unintended orphan pages remain in the active inventory.
- Cluster overlap is documented and managed through bridges or scope rules.
- Graph-health metrics can be reproduced.

### Phase 3 Exit Criteria

- Priority authority pages meet the project's depth and evidence standards.
- Foundational terms are explained consistently.
- Authority pages connect to relevant clusters and bridge concepts.
- FAQs address verified user needs without padding.
- Editorial, factual, link, and technical checks pass.
- Review and refresh dates are assigned.

### Phase 4 Exit Criteria

- Priority visual assets meet educational and brand standards.
- Labels, symbols, relationships, and directions are verified.
- Accessible alternative text and captions are supplied where needed.
- Files are optimized, named, stored, and versioned consistently.
- Reusable visuals are indexed for discovery.
- Page performance remains within project targets.

### Phase 5 Exit Criteria

- A repeatable reporting cadence is operating.
- Priority decisions reference defined metrics and qualitative evidence.
- Experiments have hypotheses, success criteria, and review dates.
- Refresh, expansion, consolidation, and retirement actions are tracked.
- Results feed back into graph, authority, and visual planning.
- Data collection respects applicable privacy and consent requirements.

### Phase 6 Exit Criteria

- Each derivative has a documented audience and purpose.
- Source-to-output traceability is maintained.
- Quality, accessibility, rights, and platform checks pass.
- Distribution and maintenance responsibilities are assigned.
- Results are measured against format-specific objectives.
- Lessons are recorded for future expansion cycles.

## 7. Daily Operating Rhythm

Daily work should protect quality while keeping production moving.

1. Review current priorities, blockers, and newly available data.
2. Select one clearly scoped outcome with an explicit definition of done.
3. Confirm the relevant standards, source materials, and dependencies.
4. Produce or revise the asset in the authoritative workspace.
5. Run automated validation appropriate to the change.
6. Conduct human review for accuracy, clarity, safety, and tone.
7. Build or render the affected output when required.
8. Record the result, unresolved questions, and next action.
9. Commit changes in a traceable unit when version control is used.
10. Publish only after required approvals are complete.

Daily production should avoid mixing unrelated changes in one work unit. Urgent work should still satisfy minimum safety and validation controls.

## 8. Weekly Operating Rhythm

The weekly rhythm coordinates production with system health.

### Weekly Review

- Review completed assets against planned outcomes.
- Inspect failures, corrections, and recurring review comments.
- Check technical health, broken links, duplicates, and publishing errors.
- Review graph gaps, weak pages, and authority-page needs.
- Examine meaningful search and user signals without overreacting to noise.
- Rebalance priorities based on value, risk, effort, and dependencies.

### Weekly Planning

- Choose a small set of measurable deliverables.
- Assign ownership and review responsibilities.
- Identify research, design, engineering, or legal dependencies.
- Reserve capacity for maintenance and quality fixes.
- Record decisions that alter standards or scope.

### Weekly Deliverables

- Updated priority queue.
- Completed and validated knowledge assets.
- Short health report or dashboard update.
- Logged decisions and unresolved risks.
- Clear starting point for the next work cycle.

## 9. Monthly Operating Rhythm

Monthly review evaluates whether the project is compounding in the intended direction.

### Portfolio Review

- Compare production against strategic objectives.
- Review authority coverage by category and cluster.
- Inspect knowledge-graph connectivity and bridge entities.
- Assess visual asset coverage and reuse.
- Review content freshness, corrections, and maintenance burden.
- Evaluate channel performance and audience feedback.

### System Review

- Audit documentation for missing or outdated standards.
- Review automation reliability and validation coverage.
- Inspect accessibility, performance, privacy, and security findings.
- Compare estimated and actual production effort.
- Identify repeated work that should become a template, tool, or reusable asset.

### Monthly Decisions

- Continue, revise, pause, merge, or retire initiatives.
- Select authority and graph priorities for the next month.
- Approve experiments with clear success criteria.
- Allocate resources among maintenance, growth, visuals, and expansion.
- Update the roadmap when evidence changes the sequence of work.

## 10. Authority Strategy

Authority pages are the most complete and dependable explanations of foundational entities within a project. They should be selected for strategic importance, user need, graph centrality, and long-term relevance. An authority page should define its subject, explain context, resolve ambiguity, and connect related concepts. Depth should come from useful distinctions, evidence, history, applications, examples, limitations, and questions—not repetition. Every authority page should have a clear search intent and a broader educational purpose. Authority pages should link contextually to supporting entities and receive meaningful links from relevant clusters. They should not become indiscriminate link directories. Review authority pages on a scheduled basis and after material changes in the domain. Track source quality, corrections, user questions, and internal-link performance. Use authority pages as source material for diagrams and derivative formats only after editorial validation.

## 11. Knowledge Graph Strategy

The knowledge graph represents entities and the meaningful relationships among them. Each entity should have a unique identity, stable name, defined scope, and appropriate location. Clusters should include a primary hub, supporting entities, and links to adjacent clusters where genuine relationships exist. Bridge entities reduce isolation between categories without collapsing distinct topics into one page. Internal links should help users understand sequence, contrast, dependence, origin, application, or association. Link density should reflect usefulness rather than a universal numeric target. Graph audits should check duplicate entities, broken links, orphans, weak inbound support, excessive outbound lists, and category overlap. Merge candidates should be evaluated by intent, scope, evidence, and maintenance burden—not keyword similarity alone. New entities should be added only when they contribute distinct value and can be connected responsibly. Graph decisions should be recorded in a reusable entity or content model where practical.

## 12. Visual Knowledge System

Visual assets should explain structure, sequence, comparison, geography, symbolism, or relationships that text alone communicates less efficiently. Official categories may include hero illustrations, knowledge diagrams, relationship maps, timelines, comparisons, symbol illustrations, flow diagrams, maps, and icon sets. Every visual must have a stated educational purpose and designated source page. Design standards should define palette, typography, spacing, hierarchy, labels, dimensions, export formats, and accessibility requirements. Domain accuracy takes precedence over visual novelty. Diagrams must be reviewed for incorrect labels, symbols, orientation, arrows, sequence, and implied causality. Reusable diagrams should be maintained as shared assets and indexed by topic and permitted use. Version history should distinguish drafts, revisions, and approved production files. Alternative text should communicate the visual's function without duplicating nearby prose unnecessarily. Image performance should be managed through appropriate dimensions, compression, modern formats, and loading behavior.

## 13. AI Collaboration Model

AI systems are production partners operating within documented human governance.

### Human Responsibilities

- Set strategy, scope, standards, and risk tolerance.
- Approve sensitive interpretations and final publication.
- Exercise factual, ethical, cultural, and brand judgment.
- Resolve ambiguity that materially changes project direction.
- Maintain accountability for outcomes.

### Planning AI Responsibilities

- Analyze opportunities and dependencies.
- Draft plans, briefs, standards, and review criteria.
- Support research synthesis and knowledge modeling.
- Identify inconsistencies, gaps, and candidate improvements.
- Present uncertainty and assumptions clearly.

### Implementation AI Responsibilities

- Execute tightly scoped content, code, data, or documentation changes.
- Preserve project conventions and change boundaries.
- Run validation, tests, builds, and audits when authorized.
- Report modified assets, results, failures, and residual risks.
- Avoid unrelated changes and unapproved external actions.

### Specialist AI Responsibilities

- Generate or revise images, diagrams, translations, metadata, or structured data within specialist standards.
- Preserve provenance and source relationships.
- Submit outputs to domain-appropriate review.
- Record model limitations when they affect confidence.

AI-generated work should never bypass the same quality gates applied to human-produced work. Prompts, inputs, and outputs should exclude confidential or regulated information unless approved systems and controls are in place.

## 14. Quality Assurance Workflow

Quality assurance is a sequence of prevention, verification, review, and release controls.

### Before Production

- Confirm scope, owner, audience, and acceptance criteria.
- Verify that the asset is not a duplicate.
- Identify authoritative sources and required expertise.
- Check applicable templates, policies, and risk constraints.

### During Production

- Preserve source meaning and declared uncertainty.
- Follow naming, taxonomy, structure, and accessibility standards.
- Use internal links only when destinations exist and relationships are relevant.
- Record questions that require human judgment.

### Automated Validation

- Check schemas, frontmatter, slugs, and required fields.
- Check broken internal and related links.
- Detect unintended orphan pages and duplicate identifiers.
- Run formatting, type, test, build, and rendering checks as applicable.
- Verify asset dimensions, formats, naming, and file sizes where applicable.

### Human Review

- Review factual accuracy and source quality.
- Review clarity, completeness, tone, and audience fit.
- Review cultural, legal, ethical, accessibility, and safety concerns.
- Confirm that visuals and relationships do not imply unsupported claims.

### Release

- Confirm approvals and validation results.
- Publish through the controlled workflow.
- Verify production output and discoverability.
- Record version, date, owner, and follow-up requirements.

### Post-release

- Monitor errors, feedback, usage, and search behavior.
- Correct material issues promptly and transparently.
- Schedule refreshes based on risk and domain volatility.
- Feed recurring findings back into standards and automation.

## 15. Data Driven Growth

Data Driven Growth combines quantitative evidence with editorial and domain judgment. Relevant signals may include impressions, rankings, clicks, engagement, completion, conversions, navigation paths, link centrality, accessibility defects, freshness, and support questions. Metrics must be interpreted in context. High traffic does not prove quality, and low traffic does not prove low value. Define a small metric set for each project objective. Separate leading indicators, outcome metrics, and guardrail metrics. Prioritize opportunities by expected user value, strategic fit, confidence, effort, risk, and reuse potential. Use controlled experiments where causal understanding matters and sample size permits. Document hypotheses before reviewing outcomes. Avoid changing strategy in response to isolated daily fluctuations. Combine search data with graph analysis to distinguish missing demand coverage from weak internal support. Use refreshes, consolidation, and improved distribution before assuming that more pages are required. Respect privacy, consent, retention, and platform requirements throughout measurement.

## 16. Anti-patterns (Things Not to Do)

- Do not publish disconnected pages merely to increase content count.
- Do not create duplicate entities with alternate slugs to target near-identical phrases.
- Do not expand a cluster without a hub, scope, and linking plan.
- Do not use internal links as keyword decoration.
- Do not overload related lists until they lose navigational value.
- Do not confuse word count with depth or authority.
- Do not add FAQs that repeat the article or invent demand.
- Do not present AI output as verified expertise without review.
- Do not conceal uncertainty, disputed interpretations, or source limitations.
- Do not publish unsupported medical, legal, financial, or safety claims.
- Do not use visuals as decoration when they add no understanding.
- Do not generate diagrams without checking labels, relationships, and orientation.
- Do not optimize for search engines at the expense of reader comprehension.
- Do not chase trends before the evergreen foundation is maintainable.
- Do not build derivatives that cannot be traced to an authoritative source.
- Do not automate a broken or undocumented workflow.
- Do not collect data without a defined purpose and appropriate safeguards.
- Do not allow dashboards to replace qualitative review.
- Do not leave time-sensitive assets without ownership or review dates.
- Do not change standards silently.
- Do not mix unrelated modifications in a single implementation unit.
- Do not skip validation because a change appears small.
- Do not expand faster than the project can review and maintain.
- Do not preserve obsolete assets solely because they already exist.

## 17. Long-term Evolution

KAPF is designed to support projects from their first structured pages to mature, multi-format knowledge platforms. Early projects should keep the framework lightweight while preserving its essential controls. As the asset inventory grows, manual checks should become reproducible audits and targeted automation. As teams grow, informal expectations should become explicit ownership, service levels, and approval paths. As distribution grows, the authoritative source must remain identifiable across formats and channels. Mature projects may add structured entity stores, content APIs, localization systems, rights management, provenance records, and automated asset registries. The framework should evolve through backward-compatible guidance where possible. Major changes to phase definitions, governance, or quality standards should produce a new framework version. Project-specific operating manuals may extend KAPF but should identify every intentional exception. Lessons from one project should be generalized only after confirming they apply beyond its domain and audience. The long-term objective is a portfolio of trustworthy knowledge systems that can share methods without losing subject-specific integrity. KAPF succeeds when quality compounds, maintenance remains practical, and each new output strengthens the underlying knowledge base.
